⚡ A machine-learning detective story

Can a computer spot a broken electricity meter?

Buildings write down how much electricity they use every hour. Sometimes the number is weird. We taught four computer "detectives" to find the weird ones, then held a fair contest to see who is best.

We trained four classic machine-learning models to flag anomalous hourly meter readings, tested each on five different train/test splits, tuned them, and compared them honestly. Everything below is built from the real results.

A leakage-controlled benchmark of Random Forest, Isolation Forest, Decision Tree and XGBoost on the LEAD smart-meter dataset: 5 stratified splits × 4 models, per-split hyperparameter search on the train portion, 20+ metrics, reproducible end to end.

1 · The idea

What is an "anomaly"?

A normal day looks like a gentle hill: quiet at night, busy in the daytime. An anomaly is when the meter suddenly does something silly, like jumping super high or getting stuck at zero.

An anomaly is a reading that does not fit the building's own normal pattern: a spike, a sudden drop, or a meter stuck at one value. It usually means a fault, a leak of energy, or a broken sensor.

LEAD labels are contextual: a reading is anomalous relative to that building's typical profile for the hour, weekday and season, not relative to the global distribution. That distinction decides which models can work.

3 · The data

Rare needles in a big haystack

Out of 400 readings…

Each dot is a reading. Red dots are anomalies. This imbalance is the reason "accuracy" can fool you (next section).

60 clues per reading

For every reading the computer also knows things like the time, the weather and how big the building is.

Each reading comes with building details, weather, calendar features, and the building's usual level. We added six new clues (green) that say how far a reading is from that building's own normal.

Kaggle's train_features.csv (metadata, weather, lags, ASHRAE-winner target encodings) plus engineered deviation features: log reading minus the building's typical log reading per hour / weekday / month, their max absolute value and a zero-reading flag. Composite keys that encode building_id are dropped.

4 · The accuracy trap

A lazy detective can score 98%

Imagine a detective who always says "nothing is wrong". Almost everything really is fine, so they are right almost every time, but they never catch a single problem! That is why we need smarter scores.

A model that always answers "normal" is right about 98% of the time and catches zero anomalies. So we judge models on precision (when it raises an alarm, is it real?), recall (how many real anomalies it catches) and F1 (both together).

With a ~2.1% positive rate, accuracy is dominated by true negatives. Headline metrics are F1, PR-AUC, MCC, precision and recall; accuracy is reported only to show the gap.

Accuracy
Precision of 100 alarms, how many are real
Recall of 100 real anomalies, how many are caught
F1 the balance of both
100 real anomalies: ● caught ● missed
